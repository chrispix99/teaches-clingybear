Evidence-first BLM, land, and genealogy research.

This lesson is the channel’s proof of rigor: evidence-first research, demonstrated on BLM land records and genealogy. The rule is the title — receipts or it didn’t happen. The lesson, step by step

Climb the source ladder

Rank every source before you trust it: primary record at the top, then authoritative database, then reputable secondary source, then community lead, and unsourced claim at the bottom. A BLM record beats a forum post. A marriage certificate beats a family-tree hint. The ladder decides how much weight a claim carries.

Turn a broad hunt into a research table

Vague hunting becomes a table with columns: candidate, location, distance, ownership type, mine evidence, source, uncertainty, next verification. Every row is a decision waiting for evidence, and the “uncertainty” column is mandatory — it is what keeps the table honest.

Apply the same pattern to genealogy

A proven marriage certificate is not an inferred family-tree link. The lesson shows both side by side: one claim you can cite, one you can only suspect. Teaching viewers to feel the difference is the whole point — rigor is a sensation before it’s a skill.

Practice negative-claim discipline

“I did not find it in the sources checked” is different from “it does not exist.” The first is honest and useful; the second is a claim you can’t support. Name the sources you checked and stop there.

Make the assistant show its work

Ask it to quote the relevant line, preserve the source link, and explain any contradiction — in its own words, not yours. A summary without a quoted line is a rumor with good formatting.

Keep a correction log

When new evidence reverses a conclusion, update the record and say what changed: old conclusion, new evidence, new conclusion. The log is what lets a research project survive being wrong, which every real project will be at least once.

Plan the physical follow-up — without assuming permission

Research ends at the trailhead. The lesson plans a prospecting or camping visit — access, weather, route, equipment, land-status checks — and draws the hard line: a planning assumption is not permission to enter. Verify land status before boots hit ground.

Worked example

A candidate parcel row in the research table: location and distance from Spokane, ownership type pulled from the BLM record (unpatented claim, not fee land), mine evidence from a historical database, source links preserved, uncertainty flagged (“access road status unverified”), next verification named (“county recorder + on-site check”). One month later, new evidence shows the access assumption was wrong — the correction log records the reversal, and the trip plan changes before anyone drives out. That’s the system working.

Your takeaway

  • Rank sources on the ladder before trusting them: primary record first, unsourced claim last.
  • Turn hunts into tables — candidate, evidence, source, uncertainty, next verification.
  • “Not found in the sources checked” is honest; “it doesn’t exist” is a claim.
  • Make the assistant quote the line, keep the link, and explain contradictions.
  • Log corrections explicitly; a planning assumption is never permission.

The BRAVE lens

Every episode runs through the BRAVE method: brief the outcome, reveal relevant context, authorize the next action, verify the evidence, and evolve the system.
